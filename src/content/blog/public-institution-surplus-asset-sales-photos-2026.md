**To photograph surplus assets in a way that holds up, use the same fixed set of shots for every item, keep the asset tag in frame, show wear and damage openly, and name every file after the asset number.** Then keep the originals in one archive, with one spreadsheet row per item. That is the whole system. The rest of this guide shows how to run it for a school clearing a storeroom, a hospital retiring equipment, or a public body or company selling off scrap and old stock.

*Last verified: November 2026. This guide is general good practice for photos and records. It is not legal, tax or procurement advice. Sales by public bodies, associations and foundations are usually governed by their own regulations and internal rules, so follow your own sale rules and those of the relevant authority or sale platform. We have not stated any legal threshold or procedure here on purpose.*

## Why institutional surplus sales need a different photo approach

A seller of new products wants glamour shots. An institution selling used items needs the opposite: **honest, comparable and traceable** photos. Three things set these sales apart.

- **The buyer often cannot inspect first.** The photo is the inspection. Online auction listings commonly carry a notice that photos may not be an exact representation of the item and that the written description governs, which is why photos and descriptions must agree.
- **Someone may review the sale later.** An auditor, board, inspector or a disappointed bidder may ask what exactly was sold and in what condition.
- **Hundreds of items, many photographers.** Without a rule, every employee shoots differently and file names such as IMG_4821.jpg mean nothing a month later.

## A worked scenario (hypothetical)

Take, for example, a vocational school that is replacing a workshop: about 400 items, ranging from workbenches and drill presses to office chairs, 30 monitors and a pallet of mixed cables. The school wants to sell by lots. Two staff members have two afternoons and the school's phones.

Without a system they end up with 2,000 unlabelled photos. With one, they do this:

1. Print asset-number labels for items that lack one, or use the existing inventory tag, and stick it where it is visible.
2. Set up a simple shooting spot (a wall or floor area with even light) and shoot every item in the same order.
3. Rename and sort files on the day, not later.
4. Build one sheet, one row per item or lot, with the asset number as the key.
5. Publish only the cropped copies; archive the originals untouched.

## The shot list: what to photograph for every item

| Shot | Purpose | Applies to |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Overview, front three-quarter | Lets the buyer recognise the item | Every item |
| 2. Asset tag / serial plate in frame | Links photo to record | Every item that has one |
| 3. Back or side | Shows ports, labels, hidden wear | Equipment, furniture |
| 4. Scale reference | Shows size (a ruler or a standard object beside it) | Items where size matters |
| 5. Each defect, close-up | Honest condition: dents, rust, cracks, missing parts | Every defective item |
| 6. Working state, if tested | Screen on, motor running, with the date noted in the sheet | Electronics, machinery |
| 7. Lot overview | Shows all pieces together for a mixed lot | Lots, pallets |

Shoot in landscape unless the item is tall. Use daylight or even indoor light and avoid flash glare on glossy surfaces.

## Show condition honestly: a simple grading scale

Choose a short condition scale and use it in both the photo set and the listing text. Define each level in one sentence so staff grade the same way. For example:

| Grade | Meaning (your own wording) | Photos required |
|---|---|---|
| A | Works, only light cosmetic wear | Overview + tag |
| B | Works, visible wear | Overview + tag + wear close-ups |
| C | Works with a fault, or incomplete | Overview + tag + every fault |
| D | Not working, for parts or scrap | Overview + tag + reason it fails |

The scale above is only an example; adapt it to your own rules. The key is that **the grade, the description and the photos tell the same story.** Never edit out damage and avoid using the background-removal or enhancement tools on used items, because the buyer should see the real object in its real state.

## Naming files by asset number

File names are your cheapest control. Put the identifier first, keep the format fixed, and avoid spaces.

| Example file name | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| 2024-0173_overview.jpg | Asset 2024-0173, main shot |
| 2024-0173_tag.jpg | The asset tag, as proof of identity |
| 2024-0173_defect-1.jpg | First defect close-up |
| LOT-07_overview.jpg | Lot 7, group shot |
| LOT-07_item-03.jpg | Third piece inside lot 7 |

Rules that keep this working: use the inventory number exactly as it appears in your register, keep leading zeros so lists sort correctly, use lower-case suffixes, and never reuse a number. If an item has no asset number, create a temporary one (for example TMP-0001) and record it in the sheet.

## One sheet, one row per item

Keep the photo set and the listing in sync with a single CSV or spreadsheet. A minimal column set might be:

| Column | Example |
|---|---|
| asset_no | 2024-0173 |
| description | Pillar drill, 230 V, with chuck key |
| condition_grade | B |
| defects | Paint chipped on base; belt cover missing |
| tested | Yes, date noted |
| lot_no | LOT-07 |
| photo_files | 2024-0173_overview.jpg; 2024-0173_tag.jpg |
| photographed_by / date | initials, 2026-11-20 |

Because the asset number is in both the file name and the sheet, the listing, the sale record and the archive can be cross-checked in seconds. This also makes bulk upload simple on any platform that accepts a spreadsheet; the same thinking applies as in [bulk CSV and XLSX imports](/blog/bulk-csv-xlsx-import-46-marketplaces-2026).

## Protect personal data in photos

Institutional spaces are full of personal data you did not mean to photograph. Before anything is published:

- **Faces and people** in the background: crop or cover them.
- **Licence plates** on vehicles and in car parks: cover them in the published copy.
- **Documents, files, labels and whiteboards**: patient, student, employee or customer names often sit in plain sight. Remove the documents before shooting where you can.
- **Screens and hard drives**: turn screens off. Wipe storage devices before sale according to your own IT policy; a photo of a login screen is also a security problem.
- **Location data** embedded in the image files: check whether your phone writes it, and remove it from published copies if you do not want the location shared.

Data-protection rules differ by country (for example GDPR in the EU or KVKK in Turkey), and your institution may have its own policy. Ask your data-protection officer if you are unsure. Keep the unedited originals only in your internal archive, with restricted access.

## Build an archive that survives an audit

An audit-ready photo archive does not need special software, only discipline.

- **One folder per sale or lot**, named with the date and sale reference.
- **Originals untouched**; publishable copies in a separate subfolder.
- **The sheet saved next to the photos**, exported to PDF or CSV when the sale closes.
- **Who shot it and when** recorded in the sheet.
- **Retention period** set by your own records policy, not by this article.
