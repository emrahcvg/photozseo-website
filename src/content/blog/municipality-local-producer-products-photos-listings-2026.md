**To list local producers' products online, a municipality needs three things before it needs a camera: a shared visual standard, a product information form that every producer fills in, and a file naming rule.** With those in place, a women's cooperative with a dozen jars of preserves and a family dairy with two cheeses can sit in the same catalog and look like part of one coherent regional offer. Without them, you get 40 different backgrounds, 40 naming habits and listing pages nobody can compare.

*Last verified: November 2026. This is a practical photo and listing guide. It is not legal advice, and it does not describe any municipality's sales, tender or procurement rules; follow your own legislation. Marketplace image rules change, so check each platform's current guidelines where you list.*

## What is the actual problem in a multi-producer catalog?

The scenario below is hypothetical, to show the shape of the work. A district municipality runs a local products programme with 35 producers: a women's cooperative (jams, pickles, dried herbs), two honey keepers, an olive oil press, a handicraft group (woven bags, felt), and some small bakers. They want one online presence and, for those who wish, listings on regional or national marketplaces.

The typical failures are predictable:

- every producer sends phone photos with different backgrounds and light
- product names are written four ways ("Thyme", "dried thyme 50g", "Kekik", "KEKIK 50 GR")
- the same product appears with different weights across pages
- photos are named `IMG_2231.jpg` and nobody knows whose they are

## Step 1: Write a one-page visual standard

Keep it to one page that a cooperative member can follow without training.

| Rule | Standard (example, adjust to your own) |
|---|---|
| Main image | Whole product, plain light background, centred, nothing else in frame |
| Second and third images | Back label with ingredients, close-up of texture or craft detail |
| Lifestyle image | One, optional, simple setting, same style for all producers |
| Light | Window daylight or two soft lamps, no flash |
| Orientation | Same aspect ratio for all main images (decide once) |
| Props | Only when they explain use (a spoon for honey), never another brand |
| Logos and text | No added watermarks; the product label shows what it shows |
| Retouching | Fix brightness and crop only; do not change colour or shape of the product |

Choose the aspect ratio by looking at the strictest marketplace you plan to use, then keep a larger master. Check that platform's current image page for numbers rather than relying on a figure from an article.

## Step 2: Collect product facts before photographing

Photos and text go wrong together when they are made separately. Use a shared sheet (a spreadsheet works).

| Column | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Producer code | Short stable ID; becomes the file name prefix |
| Product name (local and one other language if needed) | Same wording on photo label, sheet and listing |
| Net weight or volume | Must match the pack in the photo |
| Ingredients / material | Needed for the listing; also check the label matches |
| Variant | Flavour, size, colour |
| Origin / village | Supports the regional story |
| Certifications or registrations the producer holds | Only list what the producer can document |
| Photo consent for people | Yes/no, for any producer or workshop images |

Do not put a claim in a listing that the producer cannot prove. If a product has a registered geographical indication or organic status, the producer's own documents decide, not the catalog.

## Step 3: Run a shared photo day

For a programme of this kind a single "photo day" per district is usually more efficient than asking each producer to shoot alone.

1. Set up one table near a window with a plain backdrop and a tripod.
2. Print the shot list: front, back label, one detail, one in-use if wanted.
3. Hold a number card (producer code) in the first frame of each batch, so batches are easy to sort.
4. Photograph each producer's products together. Keep the same camera distance.
5. At the end, do a quick review on a laptop or tablet before the producer leaves, and re-shoot on the spot.

For items that cannot travel, such as large crafts or fresh goods, a trained volunteer or staff member can visit with the same kit.

## Step 4: Name and organise files

A naming rule saves hours later.

| Pattern part | Example |
|---|---|
| Producer code | `p014` |
| Product | `olive-oil` |
| Size/variant | `500ml` |
| View | `front`, `label`, `detail` |
| Full name | `p014-olive-oil-500ml-front.jpg` |

Use lowercase, hyphens, no spaces and no special characters. A descriptive file name is also a small help to search engines on your own site.

## Step 5: Build producer profiles, not just product cards

Buyers of regional products often buy the story. Keep product images uniform, and put identity in a profile card:

- producer or cooperative name and place
- a short two- or three-sentence story
- a consented portrait or workshop image
- a link or contact route chosen by the producer

Always ask for consent for photographs of people, and decide with each producer what contact details are published.

## Pre-publish checklist for the catalog

- [ ] One-page visual standard shared with all producers
- [ ] Every product has a row in the fact sheet
- [ ] Weights, names and variants match between pack, sheet and listing
- [ ] Main images share ratio, background and framing
- [ ] File names follow the rule; no `IMG_` leftovers
- [ ] Consent recorded for any people images
- [ ] No unprovable claims (organic, protected origin, health) in text
- [ ] Platform image rules re-read on the live platform page
- [ ] Legal department confirmed how the municipality may take part
