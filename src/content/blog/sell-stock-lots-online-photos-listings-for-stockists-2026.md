**The fastest way to sell hundreds of surplus items online is to stop treating them as hundreds of product shoots.** Sort the stock into tiers, photograph each tier at a different depth, name every file with your stock code, and list commodity items as lots. A stockist with a warehouse full of mixed goods earns by moving volume, so the photo workflow has to cost minutes per item, not an hour.

This guide is for people who buy stock, liquidation pallets, end-of-line goods or overruns and resell them. It is not about brand-new single products with a manufacturer catalog. The examples below are hypothetical.

## What does a stockist's photo problem actually look like?

Take an example: you bought a 400-item lot from a retailer closing down. There are kitchen gadgets, boxed small appliances, seasonal décor, a few returned items with dented boxes, and 60 identical sets of cable organizers. You have a table, a phone and a weekend.

Three things make this different from normal e-commerce:

- **Nothing has a clean catalog.** Many items have no usable supplier photo, no SKU you control and sometimes no barcode.
- **Condition varies inside one lot.** Two identical items can be new, boxed-damaged or used.
- **The unit economics are thin.** If a $6 item takes ten minutes to list, you lose money.

## How should you tier the stock before shooting?

Decide the photo depth once, before you pick anything up.

| Tier | Typical item | Photos | Listing form |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: single high-value | Appliance, branded tool, anything over your own price threshold | 5 to 7 (front, back, label, in-use, damage, accessories) | Single listing |
| B: standard singles | Mid-priced items, one or few pieces | 2 to 3 (front, label or barcode, flaw if any) | Single listing |
| C: commodity multiples | 60 identical organizers | 1 to 2 for the whole batch | One listing with quantity |
| D: low-value mixed | Odd small items, décor, accessories | 1 group photo per lot plus a contents table | Lot or bundle |

The threshold between A and B is your own number. A simple rule is to compute what an hour of your time is worth and compare it to the item's expected margin.

## How do you photograph 400 items without it taking a week?

1. **Build one station.** A table, a sheet of plain white or light grey sweep paper, a lamp or window light from one side, and a phone on a stand or a pile of books. Do not move it.
2. **Shoot by shelf, not by listing.** Take items in the order they sit, so the physical flow is smooth. Sort out the listing structure afterwards.
3. **Start each item with a stock-code card.** Write the code on a card or paper and put it in the first frame. Later, if a file name goes wrong, the photo still tells you what it is.
4. **Use the same distance and angle for the same tier.** Consistency lets you batch-crop later.
5. **Shoot the flaw immediately.** When you spot a scratch or dent, photograph it then. You will not remember which item it was.
6. **Review on a computer or iPad the same day.** Blurry frames are easier to catch while the stock is still on the table.

A phone is enough. What matters is steady light and a repeated setup. If you use an iPhone, a template camera helps you repeat the same angles item after item.

## Lots and pallets: how do you photograph a group honestly?

A lot is one purchase. The buyer takes everything, so the photo and the description together are the contract. Marketplace help pages on bundles generally say the same thing: the photo should show what is included, and props that are not included can mislead.

- **Lay items flat in a grid** and shoot from directly above. Do not stack.
- **Show scale.** Put one item in front, or a ruler in the corner.
- **Photograph a few representative items closer**, then list the rest in a table.
- **For identical multiples**, photograph one item plus the full batch together, and state the exact count.
- **For pallets**, photograph each face of the pallet, the top layer and the shipping label, and say plainly that contents beyond the visible layer are as stated in your manifest.

| Lot type | Main photo | Extra photos | Description must state |
|---|---|---|---|
| Identical multiples (60 organizers) | Full batch grid | One item close-up | Exact count, condition, packaging |
| Mixed small items | Flat-lay of everything | Close-ups of the best 3 | Item-by-item table with quantities |
| Mixed-condition lot | Flat-lay, damaged items to one side | Close-ups of each flaw | How many new, boxed-damaged, used |
| Pallet | Wrapped pallet from each face | Top layer, label | Manifest source, what is guaranteed |

## How do you show damaged boxes, used goods and returns honestly?

Honesty is also the cheapest return policy. Buyers of stock lots expect imperfection but not surprises.

- **Damaged packaging, intact product:** show the damaged box side, then the product out of the box. Write "box damaged, product unused" only if it is true.
- **Used or returned items:** show every visible wear mark. eBay's picture policy states that stock photos are not allowed for used, damaged or defective items, so use your own photo.
- **Missing parts:** photograph what is included and list what is missing.
- **Do not edit away flaws.** Background cleanup is fine; retouching a scratch is not.
- **Do not add borders, watermarks or text** on the image on eBay; its picture policy prohibits them.

Your own photo has a second benefit: it proves condition if a buyer disputes it later.

## How should you name files with stock codes and barcodes?

Your stock code is the key that ties the shelf, the spreadsheet and the photo together. Put it first.

| Element | Example | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Stock code | A07-0142 | Unique, sorts correctly |
| View | front, back, label, damage | Tells you what the photo shows |
| Sequence | 01, 02 | Keeps the main image first |
| Full name | A07-0142-front-01.jpg | No spaces, lowercase view |

Keep the barcode, title, condition and price in one spreadsheet row per stock code. If you also want search-friendly names for the public listing, create those as a second export copy and keep your internal codes in the master folder. That way, an unsold item can be found on the shelf from the photo alone.

## What is the minimum quality you can get away with?

Marketplaces differ, so check the live guidelines. The one number worth remembering for eBay is its published minimum: at least 500 pixels on the longest side. Past that, quality is practical: sharp focus, no motion blur, the whole item in frame, a plain background, and the flaw visible. Skip filters and heavy retouching.

## A stockist's pre-publish checklist

- [ ] Every item has a unique stock code visible on the shelf and in the sheet.
- [ ] Condition is stated in words and matches the photos.
- [ ] Lots list exact quantities in a table.
- [ ] Damaged items have a flaw photo.
- [ ] No watermarks, borders or promotional text on images where the marketplace forbids them.
- [ ] Files named stock code first, no spaces.
- [ ] You checked the marketplace's current image rules for the categories you sell.
